Oregon factory builds the walking robots of tomorrow: ‘These are going to be a part of everyday life’

  • Updated: Aug. 14, 2025, 3:51 p.m.
  • |Published: Aug. 08, 2025, 6:59 a.m.
By
  • Mike Rogoway | The Oregonian/OregonLive

To a robot, it might look like something from a horror film.

Parts — a battery, circuit boards and wiring —are arrayed in pieces on a white lab bench. Metallic arms and legs lay inert on nearby shelves. A steady, mysterious thump resonates in the distance.

These aren’t dismembered machines, though. These are the pieces of robots that are waiting to be born in Agility Robotics’ new, 70,000-square-foot “RoboFab” in Salem.

Agility Robotics spun out of Oregon State University a decade ago. It opened the Salem factory last year to mass produce hundreds of copies of a robot called Digit, which walks around on two legs like people do.

Digit stands 5 feet, 9 inches tall, with an aqua-green torso, metallic grey limbs, and brightly glowing rectangular eyes. Its every step makes that resonant thump, audible throughout the factory, as newly assembled models go through their trials, proving their mettle by lifting, carrying and stacking boxes weighing up to 55 pounds.

Already, Digit is lugging tubs of shapewear around a Spanx warehouse in Georgia. Agility plans to have hundreds of its robots on the job by the end of the year, taking on tough and dirty work for a variety of shipping companies and manufacturers.

Agility Robotics and its rivals in the U.S., China and many other countries are racing to prove that their robots can step from the pages of science fiction novels and do real jobs, working safely side-by-side with people.

The Oregon company plans to introduce a new version of Digit late this year, aiming to stay ahead of rivals like Tesla that are developing their own working robots.

Digit comes equipped with sensors to see the people around it, and a brain designed to keep human colleagues safe. Agility Robotics hopes to gradually integrate its robots into all parts of the human experience — from working farms to caring for seniors.

“These are going to be a part of everyday life,” said Jonathan Hurst, Agility Robotics’ cofounder and an engineering professor at Oregon State.

Being useful

Does a walking robot need a head? A torso? Arms?

Scientists at Oregon State spent years pondering these questions. Hurst and his colleagues studied how animals move and experimented with myriad designs as they sought to duplicate — mechanically — what people do naturally.

The university’s robotics lab is among the most advanced in the world and was one of the first to develop robots that could walk around like people. Oregon State showed off early versions by taking robots running around campus, sprinting around the track, climbing stairs and navigating uneven terrain.

These were academic exercises with a robot called Cassie that was nothing more than a pair of legs. Backed by federal funding, Oregon State’s researchers were demonstrating their growing understanding of locomotion.

“It’s very easy to make something that looks like a person,” Hurst said. “It’s very, very hard to make something that moves and captures the physics.”

Agility Robotics is an effort to find practical applications for the researchers’ academic concepts. The company has raised $180 million from Amazon and other backers, and Oregon State still holds a small stake in the business.

A report last spring in online tech journal The Information indicated the Salem company is raising another $400 million in an investment round that values Agility Robotics at $1.75 billion. (The company acknowledges that it’s raising money but declined to comment on The Information’s figures.)

Investment analysts at Bernstein Research predict that humanoid robots could be a $20 billion market by 2032, with more than 1 million robots walking around.

If that sounds like a lot, Bernstein’s analysts caution that while bipedal robots like Digit are visually striking, they’re not right for many jobs.

“We remain skeptical of two-legged designs for most applications,” Bernstein’s analysts wrote in a note to investors in spring. Usually, they said, it’s better to have a robot custom-designed for specific work — like the robotic arms that assist in building a car, or wheeled bins that zoom around ecommerce warehouses.

That’s the problem Agility Robotics faced as it spun out of Oregon State: What could a walking robot do better than people, or better than other robots?

Agility Robotics tried out various ideas. Package delivery didn’t work because the robot still needed a human driver to get around town. Custom-built robots were better at inspecting finished products at the end of a manufacturing line — arms and legs didn’t add much to quality control work.

So Agility turned to warehouses like Amazon’s, where packages come in all shapes and sizes. Hurst said heavy lifting and repetitive tasks emerged as the best place for Digit to take its first steps.

“The first useful thing that people want to pay money for is picking up bins and putting them somewhere,” he said.

Amazon declined to speak directly about its investment in Agility Robotics. The company said it’s excited about the potential of humanoid robots, adding that Digit and similar technologies clearly need more research before they will be widely adopted.

The first company to begin using Digit in its warehouses is GXO Logistics, which contracts with companies like Spanx to manage their inventory and shipping. Late last year, Agility struck a deal with German technology manufacturer Shaeffler Group, which invested in the Oregon company and will buy robots to use in its factories.

Customers have the option of purchasing robots from Agility Robotics or renting them for a period of time, what the company calls “robots-as-a-service.”

Agility won’t say what it charges — analysts estimate the parts that make up a humanoid robot (Digit is assembled from 5,000 pieces) could cost up to $60,000.

Safety first

The current version of Digit is its fourth, and the first with a head. Prior iterations stopped at the neck.

Agility Robotics found heads weren’t necessary for Digit to walk around and balance, so long as it had cameras elsewhere on its body to help it navigate. Digit’s brain, its motherboard, is in its torso.

But it turns out heads do matter when humanoid robots are interacting with people.

Humans need visual cues to help them understand and predict a robot’s behavior, according to Hurst, and to feel comfortable interacting with something that looks like a person — but isn’t. Agility Robotics found that giving Digit a head made people like it more.

“It tells you that the robot is on and aware,” he said. “It also indicates where it’s about to move.”

Ultimately, Hurst said, Digit needs to be attractive to people if it’s going to thrive in hotels, restaurants and in people’s homes. A decade from now, he said humanoid robots will be more visually appealing — lovable and smart, interacting with people in a natural way with brains augmented by artificial intelligence.

“It’s going to be a cross between a Star Wars droid,” Hurst said, “and a Pixar robot.”

It’s not just about curb appeal. Safety is a key consideration, too, with a robot that weighs more than 160 pounds.

“It’s definitely possible for that sort of size and weight to injure a person,” Hurst said.

Safety has been at the forefront of robots and their mythology from the start. Science fiction writer Isaac Asimov famously coined Three Laws of Robotics that prohibited robots from harming people and required them to obey human orders.

The risk from today’s robots isn’t that they’ll deliberately hurt someone, but that they might do so inadvertently. Digit isn’t very smart, Hurst said, so Agility Robotics is more concerned about whether it can comply with OSHA workplace regulations than whether it can obey Asimov’s three laws.

Digit’s fifth version will make safety central to the design, using artificial intelligence to recognize when people are nearby and to avoid doing things that could hurt them. Agility Robotics hopes the new incarnation could work comfortably alongside humans when it’s deployed sometime in 2026.

Agility Robotics won’t say how much it has spent on its Salem factory, but the Marion County assessor values the factory’s inventory and equipment at nearly $40 million. The company will receive property tax exemptions for its first three years there.

The RoboFab employs 150 (human) workers of Agility Robotics’ staff of 280. The company plans to turn out hundreds of robots in 2025 and far more than that in 2026. Hurst said there is room to grow for three more years in the current facility.

But while its research and manufacturing are in Oregon, Agility Robotics’ executives are scattered around the country — in San Diego, the Bay Area and Pittsburgh. Hurst said the company’s long-term future in Oregon depends on whether the state develops a cluster of expertise in robotics.

“We will grow where the talent is,” he said. “We’re going to need to build an ecosystem here if we’re going to have successful companies.”
